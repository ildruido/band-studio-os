# R-008 Image rights for band promo content: report

Protocol: `protocol.md`, pre-registered in commit c98d2bd on 2026-11-26. Tier: standard. Method: `docs/research/framework.md`.

**Not legal advice.** Desk research by an AI, not reviewed by a lawyer.

## Verdict

**Reshape.** One key hypothesis holds: the band can use what the app generates (H1, medium confidence, lower for the chain from provider to us to the band). The AI labelling duty and its dates are well established (it applies to us the day we launch), but that a small team can meet it cheaply is **not shown** (H3, insufficient evidence on feasibility). The third key one, uploading the band's own reference images (H2), is **insufficient evidence**, leaning mixed: the band's own use looks low-risk, but I found no authoritative source on how exposed *we* are when we store a photo, send it to an AI provider and hand back a transformed poster, and a rights sentence does not remove a photographer's statutory right. Nothing triggered a kill criterion. The reshape is a short rule set for uploads (D-044), clear wording about ownership and labelling (D-045, D-046), and a labelling spike before launch; it is not a retreat from uploads. Gig media with crowds (H5, feature L8) stays blocked until the UK side and the Italian privacy side are read properly.

## Strongest evidence against our idea

The best case that we are wrong: the law around AI images is moving and unsettled, and a one-person service sits in the worst spot. We would be the party that stores a band's photos, sends them to an AI company, and returns a poster. Photographers have real, cheap claims: a UK court ruled in 2013 that the unlicensed use of one band photo on 200 posters and 10,000 flyers would, if the photographer won at trial, earn £5,682 plus VAT (E-15); the defendant was the venue that used it. If a band uploads a friend's photo and the output keeps its look, the photographer's claim may reach us as the platform that made and delivered the poster, and "we told them to only upload their own" is a defence we have not seen tested. The AI providers' terms hand the risk back to us: OpenAI's output indemnity excludes trademark claims and anything we knowingly or negligently pass through (E-03), and Stability's terms make the customer indemnify Stability for outputs (E-04). Italy now says works made with AI tools are protected only where they result from the author's intellectual work (E-07), so a band could end up with a poster nobody owns, and a competitor could copy it. And the labelling duty applies to us the day we launch (E-12, E-13), while the one route that makes it cheap, embedded metadata, is often stripped when a poster is posted on Instagram (not verified; see "What we could not find").

## Findings

**H1: using generated images (supported, medium).** Four providers' published terms say the provider claims no ownership and passes on whatever rights it has: OpenAI ("you own the Output. We hereby assign to you all our right, title, and interest, if any", E-01, with the same in the business terms for API customers, E-02), Stability AI (E-04, and its community licence allows commercial use under USD 1M revenue, E-05), Black Forest Labs ("We claim no ownership rights in and to Your Content", E-06) and Google's Gemini API ("Google won't claim ownership over that content", E-08). None bars commercial promotional use of outputs, but the terms I read are consumer or general terms that grant rights to the account holder (for the API, that is us, not the band), so whether we can pass the rights on to the band is **untested**. The skeptic also found terms that matter for a third-party app: Stability bars reselling its Services "except as expressly approved by Stability" (S-01), Black Forest Labs bars commercial exploitation of its Services with an exception for Output and puts API use under a separate agreement (S-02), and Google's Gemini API requires paid services for EEA and UK users and bars apps aimed at under-18s (S-03). These do not trigger the kill criterion on their face, but each must be settled in the provider terms check before a provider is chosen. All four put responsibility for the output and its legality on the customer and warn that other users may get similar output. Two more providers, Midjourney and Adobe, could not be opened (403); they carry no weight here. These are generic terms: the exact API terms and any indemnity for each model must be read when the image provider is chosen (architecture spike). On ownership, the law points the other way from the terms: Italy's Law 132/2025 Article 25 adds "human" to copyright's definition of a work and protects AI-assisted works that result from the author's intellectual work (E-07, law-firm commentary; the statute's primary text was not opened by me or the skeptic); the UK still has section 9(3), which gives the person who made the arrangements authorship of computer-generated works (E-09), but the government's March 2026 report proposes removing it and no bill or date exists (E-09); EU originality is tied to human creative choices (E-09). So: use is fine, **exclusive ownership of a pure AI poster cannot be promised**, and the parts the band adds (logo, text, its own photos, its curation) are protected separately. Getty v Stability (E-19) found trade-mark infringement in a few outputs showing Getty's watermark, so a generated poster can reproduce a mark; the claimants' main copyright claims did not succeed (as reported by commentators; the judgment itself was not opened). The Court of Appeal has permission to hear a point of law and has not decided; a decision is expected in 2027 (S-05).

**H2: reference images (mixed, low).** Italian law gives the photographer the exclusive right to reproduce and distribute a photograph (Law 633/1941 art. 88), for 20 years for a *simple* photograph (art. 92) (E-14); a professional band photo is usually a creative work under art. 2 n. 7 (life of the author plus 70 years; confirmed by the skeptic), so the risk window is longer than the 20 years suggests. A person photographed on commission may publish the portrait without the photographer's consent but must pay a fair fee and credit the photographer, unless the contract says otherwise (art. 98) (E-14): so a band that paid for a shoot is usually fine, but not always free of charge. A portrait of a person may not be shown or sold commercially without consent (art. 96) (E-14), which is why only members who agreed should appear. In the UK, the *Sheldon* case shows the money at stake for promo use of an unlicensed band photo (E-15). Photographs are not special-category data merely because they show faces (GDPR recital 51, E-16). Our own exposure as a host is limited by the DSA hosting exemption while we lack knowledge of illegal content and remove it promptly on notice (E-17), which supports a warranty plus a takedown route. What is missing: whether the exemption still protects us when we transform the image and deliver a derived poster (not found), and any Italian or UK ruling on a generative-app operator. The kill criterion needed an authoritative source showing direct liability that a warranty and takedown cannot reduce; none was found, so H2 is mixed, not contradicted.

**H3: labelling (duty and dates supported; feasibility not shown).** Article 50 of the AI Act applies from 2 August 2026 (E-11, E-12, E-13). Providers of systems that generate images must mark outputs in a machine-readable format and make them detectable, "as far as this is technically feasible" and considering "the costs of implementation" (E-12). The Digital Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force 27 July 2026) added one transition: providers of generative systems **placed on the market before 2 August 2026** have until 2 December 2026 to comply with Article 50(2) (E-13). **That does not help a new product:** a system first placed on the market later has no grace, so the handoff's "no grace period" is correct and the date is moot until the pilot starts. A secondary commentary that reported a different date (2 December 2027) is the high-risk rules' date, not Article 50 (E-13 text shows both). The Commission's guidelines allow a provider to use marking solutions from upstream providers or third parties, but responsibility stays with the provider of the system (E-10 summary of the guidelines), so using a provider's built-in marking helps but does not shift the duty. Deployers (the band) must disclose a "deep fake", that is AI content that resembles existing persons, places or events and would falsely appear authentic (Art. 50(4)); for evidently artistic or creative work, a light disclosure that does not hamper the display is enough (E-12, E-10). The Commission published a final voluntary Code of Practice on 10 June 2026 whose signatories can rely on it to show compliance (E-11). Italy also makes harmful deepfake dissemination a crime (E-07, secondary). The duty and its dates are solid. What is **not shown**: that a small team can meet it cheaply. The Code of Practice text and the full guidelines were not opened, nothing records what OpenAI or Google already embed in their outputs, and whether metadata survives social-media upload is unverified. So the handoff's plan (embedded metadata plus a flag) is the right start but is unproven; a labelling spike is added (D-046).

**H4: logos, fonts, marks (insufficient evidence).** The SIL Open Font License lets fonts be used, studied and embedded freely and forbids only selling the font by itself (E-18), so open-licence fonts placed by our code are safe. Nothing was searched or found on trademark use of venue and sponsor names or on artist-style prompts, beyond provider rules that bar impersonation and require consent for real individuals (E-06). Not a blocker; unchecked.

**H5: crowd and fans (insufficient evidence, Italy partial).** Italian art. 97 lifts the consent requirement when a portrait is linked to public events, but not where publication harms the person's honour, reputation or decorum (E-14). Italian privacy rules apply separately; one secondary source says the Garante does not treat copyright rules as changed by privacy rules, and I found no Garante ruling on concert photos. The UK (ICO guidance, passing off) was not read. L8 stays blocked.

## What we could not find

- No lawyer-written or court source on whether a generative-app operator keeps the DSA hosting exemption when it transforms uploaded images.
- No ruling anywhere on a band or small business sued for using AI-generated promo art. Absence here is weak evidence of safety: such disputes are small and rarely reach court.
- The text of the Commission Code of Practice and the full Article 50 guidelines were not opened (the host blocked the PDF; only the regulator's summary was read). So how robust the required marking must be, and whether metadata alone is enough once social platforms strip it, is **unverified**.
- Midjourney and Adobe terms (403), Normattiva and EUR-Lex official pages (blocked or empty), two law-firm pages (403).
- Nothing on UK crowd or photography rules, Italian Garante guidance on event photos, trademark use of venue names, or artist-style prompts.
- Music rights (out of scope).

## Who is missing and other limits

Italian small-band and photographer voices are absent; every case found is US or UK, and most are about professional photographers against big users. Statute text for Italian law came from an unofficial mirror. Law-firm commentaries are often written to sell services (interest flag). Provider terms were read as published today and change often. Everything is dated November 2026.

## What would change our mind

- H1 flips to Contradicted if an API-specific term or indemnity for the chosen model bars end-user commercial use, or a court holds users liable for output that resembles a protected work in a way no rule of ours can reduce.
- H2 flips to Supported if a lawyer confirms the hosting exemption covers our transform-and-deliver flow; to Contradicted if a source shows direct operator liability for transformed third-party photos.
- H3 moves to Supported if the spike shows a third-party model's built-in marking or open-standard metadata satisfies the Code of Practice at our budget; to Contradicted if the Code or guidelines require a watermark we cannot get or build.
