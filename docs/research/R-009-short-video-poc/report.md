# R-009 Short promo video proof of concept and image rights: report

Protocol: [protocol.md](protocol.md), pre-registered in commit 63b5f6e on 2026-11-26. Tier: standard. Method: `docs/research/framework.md`.

## Verdict

**Reshape: template motion first; AI video later and only after a real test.** The technical spike shows a 12-second 9:16 story built from poster art, code-placed text and audio renders in about 22 seconds on a single CPU core into a 2.2 MB file, at a marginal cost near zero (a few CPU-seconds on a small server). We could not check Instagram's or TikTok's own spec pages, and the sample uses made-up art and placeholder sound, so platform fit is unproven. AI image-to-video is affordable on paper (0.25 to 0.35 USD per 5-second clip at two providers' own price pages), but the cost per usable clip is unmeasured, fans have reacted badly to AI-looking band promo, and one provider's general terms reserve training rights. An independent test found current top models can render text correctly, so "AI video always garbles text" is not supported either; we keep text in code for correctness and control, not because models cannot do it. Rights can be handled by design rules (own images and audio only, consent for faces, an AI label), but the evidence is thin and none of it is checked with a lawyer.

## Strongest evidence against our idea

The best case that we are wrong has three parts.

1. **Template motion may not be enough, and AI motion may be unusable.** A zoom on a poster is what any free app already does. If bands want real moving footage, only AI video or the band's own clips deliver it, and AI video looks cheap to fans: Stereogum and Futurism report fans calling out AI flyers and an AI teaser with garbled text (E-07, E-08). Band audiences punish AI-looking promo, and this product's principle is authenticity first.
2. **Our cost number is the cheapest possible one.** Per-second prices are for generated clips. A band will throw away several clips to get one usable one, so the real cost per usable clip is a multiple of the price list, and we measured none of it.
3. **Independent tests show the models are improving.** One independent comparison of 15 video models found Veo 3.1 rendered requested on-screen text correctly and several others did too (S-01). If text and reference fidelity keep improving, the case for deferring AI video weakens, and our rule "text always by code" is about control and correctness rather than a model limit.
4. **The rights picture rests on law-firm summaries and an unreadable Instagram page.** The Meta pages on AI labels and music for business accounts could not be opened here, so the platform rules are second-hand.

Note: a separate image-rights study, [R-008](../R-008-image-rights/report.md), ran in parallel and covers stills (reference images, ownership wording, AI labelling) at higher confidence; the rights part of this report covers video and audio only where it adds to that.

## Findings

**V1.** The spike (E-01) renders a 12 s, 1080x1920, 30 fps H.264 and AAC MP4 in about 22 seconds of wall time pinned to one CPU core, producing a 2.2 MB file. It uses ffmpeg: a slow zoom and drift on the poster art, film grain, vignette, a fade in and out, and five lines of text fading in one by one, placed by code. Code-placed text stays sharp and correct, which is the point of design choice 1 in the architecture handoff. Two practical findings: film grain at full strength made the file 84 MB (about 56 Mbit/s) until we lowered it and capped the bitrate, and a colon inside drawtext text cuts the line short unless escaped (it dropped the "00" of "21:00" in the first render). Instagram's help page says reels accept ratios from 1.91:1 to 9:16, at least 30 fps and 720 px, and recommends MP4, H.264, AAC (E-09, from search results, not opened; third-party guides agree on 1080x1920). The sample meets those settings, but we did not upload it to Instagram or TikTok (TikTok's rules were not searched). The "one core" figure was measured by pinning the process with `taskset -c 0` and `-threads 1`; the scripts do not enforce it. Cost: a render is about 22 CPU-seconds, which on a small hosting plan is a fraction of a cent (not computed from a real price; Assumption). The sound in the sample is a synthetic placeholder, not music, and the art is a made-up triangle pattern, not AI art, so this proves the pipeline and not the look.

**V2.** Runway's API price page (E-02) lists gen4_turbo at 5 credits per second at 0.01 USD per credit, so a 5 s clip is 0.25 USD and a 10 s clip 0.50 USD. fal's price page (E-03) lists Kling 2.5 Turbo Pro image-to-video at 0.07 USD per second (0.35 USD for 5 s) and MiniMax H3 Max Turbo image-to-video at 0.015 USD per second. Runway lists Veo 3.1 Fast without audio at 0.10 USD per second (E-04). Aggregator blogs quote 0.03 to 0.70 USD per second and conflict with each other; we treated them as leads (E-05). Three usable 5 s clips per campaign at the cheapest verified providers is about 0.75 to 1.05 USD of raw generation; with an assumed three attempts per usable clip (Assumption, not measured) it is roughly 2 to 3 USD per campaign, which would roughly double the architecture handoff's 1 to 3 EUR per campaign for images. At 25 campaigns a month that is 50 to 75 USD, which eats most of the 80 EUR headroom. So it fits only if used sparingly. Veo 3.1 with audio costs 0.40 USD per second (E-04), about 2 USD for 5 s, which breaks the 1 EUR line. All prices are vendor material (grade D), valid for prices.

**V3.** Vendor guides say face and object drift and flicker are real and recommend starting from an approved still (E-06). Press coverage shows garbled text in an AI teaser spotted by fans (E-07) and fan backlash to AI flyers and videos (E-08). An independent comparison of 15 models (S-01, opened) found Veo 3.1 the "clear winner" at rendering and keeping requested text, with Sora 2, Wan 2.6, PixVerse V5.5 and Pika 2.5 also rendering the text correctly, while Runway Gen-4.5 and others struggled on other aspects; the skeptic also reports a mixed picture on reference-image fidelity (S-02, not opened). So the claim that AI video garbles text is not shown for the best 2026 models. Code-placed text stays our design for exact dates and venues, but it is a choice, not proven necessity.

**V4.** Runway's general terms of use (not necessarily its API terms; its Enterprise terms reportedly do not train on customer data, unverified) say the company does not restrict commercial use of outputs and does not claim ownership, but that "Inputs and Outputs may be used by the Company to train and improve its AI models" with a licence to the company (E-11). These are the general terms of use; whether the API has different terms is unresolved. fal's terms (E-12) are a reseller's: they say third-party model terms apply and bar training competing models on third-party materials, and we found no training claim by fal itself. So one provider verified claims training rights; the second one cannot be settled from the page. The kill criterion needs two, so it is not triggered, but the support condition is not met either. Before any AI video, the provider's terms for API uploads must be checked and an opt-out or a no-training tier chosen.

**V5.** What the sources show:

- **AI labelling.** EU AI Act Article 50(2) obliges providers of systems that generate synthetic audio, image, video or text to make outputs machine-readable as artificially generated, and Article 50(4) obliges those who deploy a system to disclose deepfakes. For an evidently artistic or creative work the duty is limited to disclosing the existence of the generated content "in an appropriate manner that does not hamper the display or enjoyment of the work" (E-13, a copy of the Act's text on an unofficial site; the two quoted duties are separate paragraphs, 50(2) for providers and 50(4) for deployers). Law-firm commentary says promotional content probably does not get the lighter regime, and that a realistic synthetic depiction of a person counts as a deepfake (E-14, secondary, drafts of the Commission guidelines). Practical rule: label any AI-generated video, and do not generate realistic people.
- **Faces and likeness.** Italian copyright law article 96 says a person's portrait may not be displayed, reproduced or put on the market without their consent (E-15, opened). The public-event exception in article 97 is read narrowly according to a search summary (E-16, secondary), and a promo flyer or reel is the commercial case. UK law has no general image right; we did not research it beyond noting it (a gap). Practical rule: the person who uploads a photo warrants they have each shown member's consent, and AI video that animates a real face needs an explicit "all shown members agreed" flag.
- **Timing.** The Digital Omnibus may move the Article 50(2) marking date from 2 August to 2 December 2026, and the Commission published guidelines dated 20 July 2026 (S-03, search summaries, not opened; adoption status disputed). Our earlier "draft guidelines" wording may be stale.
- **Who owns AI output.** UK law gives computer-generated works an author, "the person by whom the arrangements necessary for the creation of the work are undertaken" (E-17, opened). The UK Government's March 2026 report reportedly prefers repealing this provision, with no timetable (S-04, search summaries, not opened), so it may not stay. Italy's Law 132/2025 adds that only human-creative works are protected, with AI-assisted works protected where a person's intellectual effort is behind them (E-18, secondary, law-firm summaries). So a band may not be able to claim copyright over a fully AI-made clip in Italy. This matters little for a gig promo but should be in the terms.
- **Music.** Instagram's in-app licensed library is for personal, non-commercial use, and some business accounts do not have it (E-19, search summary of Instagram help text; the page was not readable). Using the band's own recording is a different question, which depends on who holds the rights to the recording and the composition and whether a collecting society mandate affects it. We did not check that, and the sample uses no music. Practical rule: the product uses only audio the band uploads and warrants it may use; it never supplies music.
- **Platform labels.** Meta's help says it may require an AI label for photorealistic video or realistic-sounding audio that is digitally created or altered, not for images (E-20, search summary; Meta page not readable). Template motion from a still is not AI video and needs no such label on this reading, but the AI-generated base art still carries the AI Act marking duty from the architecture handoff.

## What we could not find

- Independent tests of image-to-video models on faces and logos with a given reference image (the one we found, S-01, tests text and consistency in a different setup; the skeptic reports more that we did not open).
- Any cost data for usable clips, as opposed to price lists.
- Meta's own help pages on AI labels, music and reel limits (the pages did not load for automated access), and TikTok's rules (not searched).
- UK likeness and music-rights law (not searched beyond UK computer-generated works).
- Whether Runway's API terms differ from its general terms, and Google's Veo terms (the Gemini price page did not load).

- Per-hypothesis counts of disconfirming queries were not logged one by one (standard tier asks for at least 2 each): the searches run were price, drift, spec, AI Act, music, label, backlash and Italian law queries, with the contrary angle found mainly through the skeptic.

Not finding these means "unknown", not "fine".

## Who is missing and other limits

Band drivers and fans are absent: fan reactions are three press anecdotes, not data. No lawyer was consulted. All prices are as of 2026-11-26, in USD, vendor pages, and the market moves monthly (one source says a Google preview model was shut down in October 2026, unverified). The spike ran on a developer machine pinned to one core, not on the real hosting plan, and used synthetic art and sound.

## What would change our mind

- V1: an upload of the sample to Instagram or TikTok fails, or the real hosting plan renders far slower than one local core.
- V3 and the AI-video deferral: a real test on a band's actual poster (spike: five image-to-video clips, scored by Ale and a bandmate for "is this us?") showing faithful look and no artefacts at under 0.35 USD per usable clip would move AI clips from "later, optional" to "next".
- V4: a provider with written no-training API terms, or a provider whose terms bar commercial use.
- V5: a lawyer saying that animating a poster that contains stylised or realistic people needs consent, or that a band's own track in a business-account story needs a licence.

## Deviations from the protocol

- 2026-11-26: Instagram's own help page for reels specs and Meta's AI-label and music pages returned no text to automated access, and the Gemini price page was rendered by script. V1's support condition ("specs checked from a primary source") is therefore met only through search summaries and third-party guides (Low weight for the spec claims). The spike result itself is first-hand. Effect: V1 stays Medium, not High.
- 2026-11-26: per the skeptic, a spec claim from an unverified source carries no weight, so V1's verdict is Mixed and its confidence Low, not "Low weight but Supported".
- 2026-11-26: the ffmpeg spike also covers the "quality" side of V1 only as a technical output; "good enough" is Ale's call on watching the sample.
