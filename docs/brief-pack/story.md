## Problem

**In plain words:** Most small bands run on volunteer effort, and usually one or two members do all the promotion next to a day job. Promoting a single gig means juggling several apps, making flyers and posts that look amateur, repeating the same chores for every show, and waiting in a group chat for bandmates to say yes. The generic AI tools that could help make it worse for this scene: they produce smooth, corporate-looking material that a grit-and-DIY scene rejects.

**Key facts**
- Promotion eats time that should go to music. In one academic survey of musicians in five countries (none of them Italy), 23% spend over half their working time on non-music tasks such as social media and fan messages. *Needs primary research: other countries, not bands like ours.*
- Self-promotion is the norm: 91% of independent musicians in one vendor survey (UK and US, all kinds of musician) promote themselves. *Provisional: vendor survey.*
- Many dread it: both younger and older artists say they find self-promotion uncomfortable. *Provisional.*
- **Counter-evidence we must keep visible:** in the largest independent-artist survey we opened, money is "more than twice as likely" as anything else to be named the biggest barrier. Promotion may rank below money, gigs and time.
- At least one US venue publicly bans AI-made flyers. Authenticity is a real risk for any generated material (see [Risks and mitigations](evidence-and-risks.md#risks-and-mitigations)).

**How sure are we:** *Needs primary research.* Desk research is consistent with the problem and nothing contradicts it, but no source is strong and almost no Italian band has been heard directly. The driver interviews that would settle it are designed and parked.

## Customer

**In plain words:** The user is **the driver**: the one band member who runs the band's promotion. In version 1 the driver is the only person who uses the product daily and the only buyer we plan for. Their **bandmates** act as reviewers: they approve or veto the material from a link on their phone, with no account.

**Key facts**
- The driver, in one line (illustrative persona, not a real person): a guitarist-singer in their late twenties in a four-piece indie-rock band in a mid-sized Italian city, with a day job, very little money and very little time. *Illustrative, built mostly on assumptions.*
- The bandmate reviewer wants to make sure the band looks right without spending time on it. *Provisional.*
- **Not for us:** solo acts and DJs; acts with a label or manager; promotion agencies and press offices (they sell the job we replace).
- Size: about 74 thousand active in-segment bands in Italy and the UK as a base estimate, within a range of about 29 thousand to 178 thousand. *Needs primary research.* See [Market](market-and-competition.md#market).
- Only about a third of the persona traits (6 of 19) have any evidence, all of it weak. The persona set is labelled speculative and is good for guiding interview questions and early design, not for pricing or go-to-market claims.
- Who pays is not known. The driver is assumed, but may split the cost with the band.

**How sure are we:** *Provisional*, leaning *Needs primary research*. The customer has not been confirmed by anyone outside this project.

## Solution and value

**In one sentence:** A phone-first app where the one active member of a band describes the band once, enters a gig, and gets a ready-to-post set of promotion material in the band's own voice, which bandmates approve with one tap.

**In 30 seconds:** You tell the app who your band is once: your style, how rough you like things, what you never want to look like. Then you tell it about your next gig. It proposes a flyer, stories for Instagram, a post and captions that look and sound like you, not like a template. You pick, tweak, and send one link to your band. They approve or veto from their phones. You download a folder of finished posters and a posting plan, and post it yourself. Nothing leaves without your band's yes.

**In detail:** the first version covers eight things:
1. Sign in with email, one band per account.
2. A light "who are we" setup: name, genre tags, how rough or clean, a "never like this" list, up to five reference images, logo and colours if you have them.
3. Gig setup: venue, city, date, time, ticket link, support acts, one line of message.
4. A pre-gig pack: a flyer (feed format plus a print file), a set of vertical stories, a post, captions and hashtags. Images only, no video.
5. Curation: pick between versions, redo one asset, edit the words.
6. Light approval: bandmates approve or veto each asset with one tap, no account; a veto leads to one revision. Nothing exports without approval.
7. Export: a ready-to-post folder, a captions file and a suggested posting schedule. The driver posts by hand.
8. Measurement behind the scenes, with no raw media sent to analytics.

**Why it is better than today:** *do more, do better, with less.* The driver gets a complete gig campaign in a fraction of the time, with output that amplifies the band's identity instead of overwriting it. **This is the claim we have not yet shown to be true**: nobody has tested whether the output feels like the band (see [Traction and validation](evidence-and-risks.md#traction-and-validation)).

**How sure are we:** the description is *Decided* (it is the approved first version). The value claim is a *Hypothesis*.

## Positioning

**In plain words:** We are the band's **creative and identity layer for a gig campaign**: the place that holds who the band is and turns a gig into a coherent set of material that sounds like them. We are **not** an "AI poster generator", and we are **not** an advertising or audience-reach product.

**Key facts**
- The closest reach competitor is Bandsintown's "Boost" (announced June 2026): it gets the show seen and makes no creative. Ale decided to treat it as an indirect competitor and a possible future partner, not a rival to beat.
- The generic "brief in, campaign out" flow is close to what Simplified and Canva already advertise for small businesses. We must not claim the generic flow as ours. If we have an edge, it is the band-specific layer: identity, grit, anti-moodboard, and approval by the band.
- **Honest scoring:** on the criteria we set before looking, our *planned* product scores about 77 out of 100 against Canva at about 79. We only pull ahead on things we hope to build (a guided campaign, speed, Italian), not on things that exist.
- Tagline: **not yet known.** See [Brand and messaging](brand-and-messaging.md#brand-and-messaging).

**How sure are we:** the position is *Decided*. That it holds against Canva and Simplified is *Needs primary research*: the blind output test is meant to show it.

## Customer-facing story

*Illustrative. No real band has used the product. Replace with a real story as soon as one exists.*

**Before.** Marta plays guitar in a four-piece band. Their gig is in nine days. She is the one who does the promotion. Tonight, after her day job, she opens a free design app, picks a template, changes the colours, and gives up at midnight with something that looks like every other flyer. She posts it in the group chat. Two people reply "nice" and one says he is not sure about the font. It takes three evenings, and the band still looks like nobody in particular.

**After.** Marta opens Band Studio OS and types one sentence: the venue, the date, the support act. The app proposes a poster that looks like the band: rough where they are rough. She taps "rougher" once. She sends a link. Luca approves from the van. The stories and captions are ready. She downloads a folder and posts. It took one sitting, and it still looks like them.

**How sure are we:** *Illustrative.* The "after" is what the first version aims for; no one has seen real output yet.
